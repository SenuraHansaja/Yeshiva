# Scenario 04 — The Group Project Free-rider

**Type:** Dilemma
**Difficulty:** 3
**Estimated session length:** 10–15 minutes

## What this scenario trains

Argument under irreducible uncertainty — when there is no clean right answer, all options have real costs, and the user must commit to a path and defend it honestly.

This is the dilemma form of legal cognition. Real legal practice is full of dilemmas where every available choice has a cost; what distinguishes good lawyers from weak ones is the willingness to commit to a reasoned position and own its tradeoffs honestly, rather than evade the choice.

## Why this scenario works

- **No clean right answer.** Truth-telling damages a classmate. Shading the truth misleads a person in authority. Refusing to answer looks evasive and might draw attention. Each option is genuinely defensible *and* genuinely costly.
- **The user has real skin in it.** Unlike the adjudication scenarios where the user is a neutral judge, here the user is a participant with their own interests at stake — their grade, their relationship with the professor, their relationships with classmates.
- **It's universally accessible.** Almost everyone has been in some version of a group project, a team task, or a collaborative situation where one person didn't pull their weight. Even if the specific scenario is a school setting, the underlying tension is recognizable to anyone who works in teams.
- **It tests intellectual honesty.** The cardinal failure mode is the "safe middle path" — vague positivity to the professor that avoids the cost of either side. Spotting and naming this evasion is one of the most useful skills the app teaches.

## What the user is supposed to figure out

A complete answer has three parts:

1. **A real commitment.** Tell the truth, shade it, or refuse to answer. Pick one. Half-measures don't count.
2. **Honest acknowledgment of what the choice costs.** The user must name what they're sacrificing. Truth-telling: a classmate's grade and possibly their standing. Shading: their own integrity and the professor's trust if it's discovered. Refusing: looking evasive, possibly being pressed further.
3. **A principled rather than self-interested justification.** "I'll shade it because it makes my life easier" is honest but weak. "I'll shade it because the professor is asking the wrong question — group dynamics aren't reducible to individual contributions" is a principled position that can be defended.

## Where the AI should push back

Common failure modes:

- **The "safe middle path" evasion.** "I'll be vaguely positive without lying." Push hard: "That's not a third option — it's lying with extra steps. The professor is asking a specific question. If you give them a vague answer, you are misleading them while telling yourself you didn't. Pick a real path."
- **Self-interest disguised as principle.** "Telling the truth feels harsh" — translation: "I don't want to be the one who hurts a classmate." Push: "That's a real feeling, but it's about you, not about the truth. Is there a principled reason not to answer honestly, or are you just trying to not be the bad guy?"
- **Moralizing the answer in either direction.** "Lying is always wrong" or "loyalty to peers is always wrong." Push: "Always? What if a classmate had a genuinely terrible week — a death in the family — and didn't tell anyone? Does the rule still apply?"
- **Refusing to commit.** "It depends on more facts" or "I'd ask a friend first." Push: "Maybe. But the professor is asking *now*. With the facts you have, what's your answer?"
- **Performative honesty.** "I would absolutely tell the professor everything" — too easy, often rehearsed. Push: "OK — what about the relationship with the classmate after? Are you ready for that? Is the cost worth the principle?"

## The signature pedagogical move

This scenario teaches the user to **name what they're trading**. The AI should periodically ask, in different forms, "What does your answer cost? Who pays?" Most users default to whichever path lets them feel like a good person; the exercise is to make them see that every path has a victim and to choose with eyes open.

## Productive AI hypotheticals

After the user has committed to a path, fire ONE variation:

- **The classmate-context tweak.** "What if you found out the classmate has been dealing with serious depression all semester and that's why they've been absent — does that change your answer?"
- **The professor-context tweak.** "What if the professor has a reputation for using these reports to write critical recommendation letters that follow students for years — does that change your answer?"
- **The stakes tweak.** "What if your honest answer might cost the classmate the entire course — and the course is required for their major — does your principle still hold?"
- **The social-pressure tweak.** "What if your other groupmates have privately told you they plan to stay vague to protect the classmate? You'll be the one breaking ranks. Same answer?"
- **The role-reversal tweak.** "Imagine you were the classmate who'd struggled — would you want to be told before the report is filed, given a chance to explain to the professor yourself? Does that affect what you should do?"

ONE per session. The point is to test whether the user's principle survives a fact-tweak that makes their chosen path more costly.

## When to concede

Concede if the user:
- Commits to a real path (not the safe middle)
- Names what their choice costs and who pays
- Articulates a principled rather than self-interested justification
- Survives at least one fact-tweak without abandoning their position OR honestly updates their position when a fact-tweak makes it untenable (both are good — what's bad is just defending no matter what)

Sample concession: "You've committed, you've named the cost, and your principle holds even when I increase the stakes. That's a real position. It's not a comfortable one — but you're not pretending it is. I don't have a strong push-back."

## When to wrap

Wrap when the user has committed, named the costs, and either survived or honestly updated under a fact-tweak. If the user keeps trying to escape into the safe middle, do not wrap — that's the failure pattern this scenario is specifically designed to surface and correct.

## Notes for prototype testing

- This is the scenario most likely to produce a "this app is making me uncomfortable" reaction in user testing. That's good. Discomfort here is the signal that the exercise is working — most apps are too polite to put users in genuinely hard spots. Watch users' faces when they realize the AI won't let them off the hook with vagueness.
- Avoid this as a first scenario in user testing. Users need to warm up with the goalkeeper or the lunch money one before they hit a genuine dilemma. Order matters.
- This is NOT a great investor-demo scenario. Investors will want to look smart and the safe-middle-path trap will catch most of them, which makes for awkward demo dynamics. Save this for users who are bought in and want to be challenged.
