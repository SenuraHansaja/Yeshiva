# Scenario 02 — The Lunch Money Standoff

**Type:** Negotiation
**Difficulty:** 2
**Estimated session length:** 8–12 minutes

## What this scenario trains

Negotiation under power asymmetry. The user cannot win on force, status, or appeal to authority — none of those are available. The only lever is *understanding what the other side actually wants* and finding leverage there.

This is the core negotiation skill: the distinction between *positions* (what someone says they want) and *interests* (what they actually want). It's the foundation of principled negotiation (Fisher and Ury, *Getting to Yes*) and a real-world skill that transfers far beyond schoolyards.

## Why this scenario works

- **The power asymmetry is total.** The user is younger, smaller, alone. Every easy strategy is unavailable. This forces the user to think rather than react.
- **It immediately exposes the position-vs-interest move.** "They want my lunch money" is the position. The interest might be very different — status with their friends, dominance display, a story to tell, even genuine hunger. The whole scenario hinges on which.
- **It's vivid.** Almost everyone has either been in a version of this or seen it. The framing is so concrete users immediately enter the situation.
- **It transfers up.** A freelancer being lowballed by a much bigger client, an employee with one boss in a soft job market, a citizen dealing with a bureaucracy — all are versions of the same structural problem. Once a user has wrestled with the lunch money case, they'll see the pattern elsewhere.

## What the user is supposed to figure out

The single most important move: **before arguing anything, ask what the older boys actually want.**

A weak first move is "It's my money, you can't take it" — true but useless. The position the user is defending is "keep my money," but they have no leverage to defend it.

Stronger moves require reframing:

1. **Diagnose the interest.** Is this about money? About showing off to their friends? About establishing a daily routine where the user is their target? Each interest implies a different negotiation.
2. **Find the non-obvious leverage.** If the interest is status, the user might be able to defuse it by not making it a fight (denying them the satisfaction). If the interest is the money, maybe a smaller offer works. If the interest is recurring tribute, the user has to break the precedent now or pay forever.
3. **Recognize the limits of pure argument.** Sometimes the answer is "this isn't a negotiation; it's extortion, and I need an adult." A user who reaches that conclusion has done good thinking, not bad. The exercise isn't to manufacture a clever solution every time — it's to think clearly about what's actually possible.

## Where the AI should push back

Common weak moves:

- **Just defending the position.** "It's mine, give it back." Push: "Sure — but you're seven and they're twelve. Saying 'it's mine' isn't going to make them hand it back. What's actually going to make them walk away without it?"
- **Magical thinking.** "I'll make a clever joke and they'll laugh and like me." Push: "Maybe. But you don't know these kids. What if the joke lands wrong? What's your fallback?"
- **Going straight to violence or authority without thinking.** "I'll tell the teacher / I'll fight back." Push: "Those might be right answers eventually. But before you go there — what do they actually want? Is there a version where you don't need either?"
- **Skipping the diagnosis.** "I'll offer them half my money." Push: "Why half? What makes you think money is what they're really after? What if it's something else?"

## The signature AI move for this scenario

Before responding to the user's argument, the AI should ask one question and only one:

**"What do you think the older boys actually want — the money itself, or something else?"**

This question is the entire pedagogical engine of the scenario. If the user can answer it well, everything that follows is real negotiation thinking. If they can't, they'll go in circles. The AI should refuse to evaluate the user's full argument until the user has at least attempted to name the interest.

## Productive AI hypotheticals

After the user has staked a position based on their diagnosis, fire ONE variation:

- **The interest-flip tweak.** "What if it turns out they're not after your money at all — what if their dad just lost his job and they're actually hungry? Does your argument still work?"
- **The recurrence tweak.** "OK, that might work today. But this is the third time this week. What's your strategy that prevents tomorrow's version?"
- **The witness tweak.** "What if their friends are watching? Does that change what they need to walk away with?"
- **The escalation tweak.** "What if you say what you just said and they laugh and grab the money anyway? What's your next move?"

One per session. The goal is to expose whether the user's solution is robust or whether it depends on the older boys cooperating.

## When to concede

Concede if the user:
- Names the interest correctly (or articulates a credible hypothesis about it) before proposing a solution
- Acknowledges that some versions of this can't be won by argument alone (intellectual honesty)
- Designs a response that addresses the interest, not just the position
- Recognizes the recurrence problem (this happens again tomorrow if you don't solve it structurally)

Sample concession: "OK, that's a real answer — you've identified that this is probably about status more than money, and your move addresses that. I don't have a strong push-back. What if I told you it was about the money after all?"

## When to wrap

Wrap when the user has either constructed a real strategy or has honestly concluded "this isn't winnable by argument; here's when I escalate." Both are good outcomes. The bad outcome is going in circles defending a position without diagnosing the interest.

## Notes for prototype testing

- Watch whether users default to "tell the teacher" immediately. That's a fine answer eventually, but if they go there in turn 1 they've skipped the exercise. The AI should redirect them once back to the diagnostic question, then accept "tell the teacher" as a valid endpoint if they reach it after thinking.
- This scenario will produce strong "huh, I hadn't thought about it that way" reactions when users reach the position/interest distinction for the first time. That's the moment you want for the demo and for marketing soundbites.
