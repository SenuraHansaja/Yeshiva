# Scenario 03 — The Disputed Birthday Party

**Type:** Adjudication
**Difficulty:** 2
**Estimated session length:** 10–15 minutes

## What this scenario trains

Adjudication — the skill of giving a reasoned ruling between two parties when both have legitimate claims. This is what judges do, what arbitrators do, what good managers do, and what mature friends are sometimes asked to do.

This is the rarest exercise type in the competitive landscape. Almost no other app trains it. It's also the closest thing to the original Talmudic case-judgment exercise — which is the source tradition the app draws on.

## Why this scenario works

- **Both parties are sympathetic.** A weak adjudication scenario has a clear villain. Here, A spent real money and emotional labor planning something kind. B was responding to a sick partner. Neither is wrong as a person.
- **Both parties have legitimate claims.** A: "I did all this for you." B: "You should have checked with me first." Both true. The user has to *rule* anyway.
- **The user can't escape with 'they're both wrong' or 'they're both right.'** Adjudication requires a decision. Mature decisions require reasoning that the loser could understand and accept, even if not enjoy.
- **It models real legal cognition.** Judges face this constantly: two parties with legitimate competing claims, no statute that obviously decides it, and the necessity of writing a reasoned opinion. The exercise replicates that intellectual demand at low emotional stakes.

## What the user is supposed to figure out

The user must reach a ruling, and the ruling must be supported by reasoning that addresses both sides. A complete answer has three parts:

1. **A ruling.** "A is mostly in the wrong" or "B is mostly in the wrong" or "the obligation falls X-on-A, Y-on-B." Some kind of decision.
2. **The principle the ruling rests on.** Why this ruling rather than another? What is the underlying rule that, if applied consistently, would produce this answer? (E.g., "the person who incurs costs on someone else's behalf without checking takes on the risk that the plan won't be wanted" — a principle a court or a thoughtful friend could state.)
3. **An honest acknowledgment of the loser's case.** A good adjudication doesn't pretend the losing side had no argument. It says "Here's why your case isn't enough *in this situation*, even though it would be in a different one."

## Where the AI should push back

Common evasion moves:

- **"They're both equally at fault."** This is non-adjudication. Push: "That's the easy answer. But if I forced you to pick, even slightly, which way would you lean and why?"
- **"It depends on more facts."** Sometimes legitimate, but often a dodge. Push: "Sure, more facts would help. But with the facts you have — what's your tentative ruling? Real adjudicators rule with incomplete information all the time."
- **Picking a side without a principle.** "B should have known A was planning something." OK — *why?* Push: "What's the rule you're applying? If I changed the facts slightly, would the same rule still produce the same answer?"
- **The principle is too narrow.** "A should have asked first specifically because the partner was sick." Push: "What if the partner wasn't sick — would A still need to ask? Is your rule actually about asking, or about something else?"
- **Ignoring the loser's case.** "B is just being ungrateful." Push: "Maybe — but B has a real point that surprises don't always feel like surprises to the recipient. Why isn't that point enough?"

## Productive AI hypotheticals

After the user has ruled, fire ONE variation that tests whether their principle holds:

- **The reciprocity tweak.** "What if A had actually checked with B last month and B said 'I'd love a surprise party someday' — does A have implicit consent?"
- **The cost tweak.** "What if A had spent $50, not $500 — does that change your ruling? If yes, why does the dollar amount matter? If no, why doesn't it?"
- **The role-reversal tweak.** "Imagine your ruling is the one that gets applied to YOU next time you plan a surprise for someone close. Are you happy with the rule?"
- **The scope tweak.** "What if it wasn't a partner — what if A had planned a surprise for B's mother and B canceled because the mother had a fight with her husband? Same ruling, or different?"
- **The information tweak.** "What if B knew A was planning *something* but didn't know what? Does that change the calculus?"

Use ONE per session. The point is to test whether the user's stated principle generalizes or only fits this case.

## When to concede

Concede if the user:
- States a clear principle that holds under at least one fact-tweak you throw at them
- Acknowledges the losing side's strongest point and explains why it loses *here* without dismissing it
- Distinguishes between "wrong" and "not entitled to remedy" — sophisticated adjudication often rules that someone behaved badly but still owes nothing, or behaved fine but still needs to make someone whole

Sample concession: "That's a real ruling. You've identified that A took on the risk by not checking, and your principle holds even when the costs are smaller. I don't have a strong push-back here. The losing side's case is real but doesn't carry the day. That's adjudication."

## When to wrap

Wrap when the user has issued a ruling, articulated the principle behind it, and survived at least one principle-test hypothetical. Going past that gets into sophistication that's beyond the scenario's scope.

If the user refuses to rule even after pressure, that's a teaching moment, not a wrap. Stay with them. The AI shouldn't accept "I just don't want to choose" — push them gently but firmly toward a decision.

## Notes for prototype testing

- Adjudication scenarios produce the most satisfying user reactions in research literature on Talmudic-style learning. Watch for users who initially resist the demand to rule, then engage with it, then express something like "huh, I see why this is actually hard." That's the scenario doing its job.
- This is a strong second scenario for the investor demo if the goalkeeper one is taken or if you want to show variety. Avoid it as a first scenario for users who seem cognitively cautious — it's harder than the goalkeeper.
