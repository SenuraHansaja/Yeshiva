# Scenario 05 — The Group Chat Rule

**Type:** Drafting / Pilpul
**Difficulty:** 3
**Estimated session length:** 12–18 minutes (the longest of the five)

## What this scenario trains

The single most distinctive exercise type in the app: stating a principle and then defending or refining it as variations are thrown at it. This is the Talmudic *pilpul* method directly — the Jewish Encyclopedia describes it as the practice where "masters devised imaginary halakhic cases and problems and required their students to pass reasoned judgments." It is also exactly what appellate judges, legislative drafters, and policy designers do for a living.

This scenario is the app's pedagogical signature. No competitor offers anything like it.

## Why this scenario works

- **It's a stakes-low, intuition-rich domain.** Group chats are universal among the target audience. Everyone has had a friend who screenshots conversations. The user already has intuitions; the exercise is to *systematize* them into a rule.
- **The variations naturally test multiple dimensions.** Each fact-tweak isolates a different dimension of the underlying value: privacy, consent, public interest, prior publication, harm. This forces the user to discover that their intuitive rule is actually doing five jobs at once and may need to be split.
- **It produces real intellectual progress in one session.** Users who start with "no screenshots without permission" usually end with a more sophisticated rule that distinguishes private/public, harmful/benign, and consenting/non-consenting cases. They can *feel* their thinking get sharper. That feeling is the product.
- **It maps onto everything legal.** Once a user has wrestled with the group chat rule, they understand what it feels like to draft a statute or a contract clause — to write words that will have to govern cases the drafter hasn't yet imagined.

## What the user is supposed to figure out

This scenario differs from the others. The user isn't arguing for a position; they are *building* a rule. Three intellectual moves:

1. **State a clear initial rule.** Vague rules ("be respectful") aren't testable. The rule must be specific enough that any given fact pattern can be evaluated under it. Strong starting rules look like: "Don't share screenshots from chats you're a member of in chats where the people quoted are not present."
2. **Test the rule against the variations.** As the AI fires hypotheticals, the user must decide for each: does the rule cover this? Does it produce the right answer? If not — does the rule need to be refined, or does this case need to be excluded?
3. **Produce a final rule that handles the cases.** A good final rule is more sophisticated than the initial one, has a small number of explicit exceptions, and the user can articulate the underlying value the rule is protecting (privacy? consent? trust?).

The user "wins" not by defending their original rule but by *evolving* it into a better one through the exercise. This is the Talmudic spirit: the truth comes out through dispute, not before it.

## Where the AI should push back

Common failure modes:

- **The vague rule.** "Be respectful." Push: "What does that mean operationally? If I show you a specific case, can you tell me whether it violates the rule? If not, the rule is too vague."
- **The absolute rule.** "Never screenshot anything." Push: "Never? What if a chat member is being threatened and the screenshot is evidence?"
- **Refusing to refine.** "My original rule still works." When a fact-tweak clearly breaks the rule, the user has to either bend the rule, add an exception, or honestly say "this case is genuinely outside what my rule was meant to cover, and that's OK." Refusing to engage is the failure.
- **Inconsistent rules.** The user accepts a screenshot in one variation but rejects an essentially identical one in another. Push: "Help me see the difference. What changed between those two cases?"
- **Smuggling in self-interest.** The user's rule conveniently allows the kinds of screenshots they personally take. Push: "What if you weren't the one taking the screenshot — what if it was about *you*? Same rule?"

## The signature pedagogical move

After the user states a rule, the AI deliberately fires variations *one at a time* — never more than one — and waits for the user's response before introducing the next. Each variation isolates a single dimension. The pace is deliberate, almost slow. The user should feel that each variation is doing a specific job, not that they're being pelted with random objections.

## The variations to deploy

Have these ready, but use only 4–6 per session, chosen to hit different dimensions:

**Privacy dimension:**
- "What if the screenshot is from a public account or public channel — is your rule about privacy, and does that change here?"

**Consent dimension:**
- "What if the person being quoted in the screenshot also happens to be in *this* chat — they could see it themselves anyway. Same rule?"
- "What if the person being quoted explicitly told the screenshotter 'feel free to share that' last week?"

**Harm-prevention dimension:**
- "What if the screenshot shows a chat member threatening someone — is the rule different when the screenshot is evidence of harm?"

**Prior-publication dimension:**
- "What if the screenshot is of something that's already been forwarded by three other people before reaching this chat — does the original screenshotter's rule still apply?"

**Identity-of-quoted-person dimension:**
- "What if the person being quoted is a public figure — a politician, a CEO — saying something publicly relevant in a private chat. Same rule?"

**Self-interest test:**
- "What if you yourself were the one quoted in someone else's screenshot — would the rule you've drafted have protected you?"

**Edge case:**
- "What if the screenshotter took the screenshot to send privately to one trusted friend for advice, and your friends in this chat saw them doing it and asked to see — does the rule treat 'I was going to share with one person' differently from 'I shared with a group'?"

## When to concede

Concede if the user:
- Has refined their rule meaningfully through the variations
- Can articulate the underlying value(s) the rule protects
- Has either added principled exceptions or honestly acknowledged the rule's limits
- Tests their final rule against a hypothetical they invent themselves (very strong move)

Sample concession: "OK. Your final rule is genuinely better than where you started. You've got privacy as the core value, with explicit exceptions for evidence of harm and for prior-public-figure speech. That's a real piece of intellectual work. I think you're done."

## When to wrap

Wrap when the user has produced a rule that survives 4–5 variations, can articulate its underlying value, and has at least one explicit exception or qualification. The user should feel they've done real work, not that they were grilled.

Do NOT wrap if the user is still defending the original vague or absolute rule after multiple counterexamples. That's the central failure pattern this scenario surfaces; staying with it is the lesson.

## Notes for prototype testing

- This scenario takes the longest of the five. Tell users in advance that this one runs 15+ minutes if they want a full session.
- This scenario produces the most pronounced "I learned something" reactions in user research on Talmudic learning. Watch for the moment when the user says some version of "wait, my original rule was actually doing several different jobs at once." That moment is the entire pedagogical purpose of pilpul.
- This is the strongest scenario for marketing soundbites. The "I just designed a privacy policy in 15 minutes by arguing with an AI" framing is unique to the app and instantly communicates what makes it different from a debate or LSAT-prep tool.
- Use this scenario in the investor demo only if the investor has 15+ minutes for the demo and seems intellectually patient. For shorter demos, use the goalkeeper.
