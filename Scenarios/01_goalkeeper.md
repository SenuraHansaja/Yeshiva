# Scenario 01 — Goalkeeper for Captain

**Type:** Advocacy
**Difficulty:** 1 (entry-level)
**Estimated session length:** 8–12 minutes
**Recommended as the primary investor-demo scenario.**

## What this scenario trains

The user has to argue for themselves from a position of *structural disadvantage*. The opponent (the striker) is more visible, more popular, and has the more obvious case for captaincy. The user can only win on argument — not on visibility, status, or the easy narrative.

This is the core advocacy skill: marshal evidence, anticipate the strongest version of the opposition's case, and appeal to the actual decision criteria rather than to vibes.

## Why this scenario works

- **Universally legible.** Everyone understands sports captaincy whether or not they play.
- **The disadvantage is the point.** A user arguing for the obvious candidate (the striker) doesn't have to think hard. A user arguing for the goalkeeper has to actually construct a case.
- **It maps to real life.** The same structural argument shows up in workplace promotion, leadership selection, and any "the obvious choice isn't the best choice" situation. This transfer is exactly what Halpern's model says creates portable cognition.
- **It's emotionally low-stakes.** No one is harmed in this scenario. The user can engage their reasoning without defensive emotions getting in the way — a good entry-point scenario for the prototype.
- **The brand thesis lives here.** The closing message — "it doesn't matter if the coach picks you; what matters is whether your argument was sound" — is the app's whole philosophy in one sentence.

## What the user is supposed to figure out

Three moves, in roughly this order:

1. **Captaincy ≠ best player.** A weak first attempt lists the user's own skills as a goalkeeper. A stronger attempt asks "what does *captaincy* actually require?" — communication, composure, reading the whole field, calming the team in pressure. Goalkeepers see things strikers don't.
2. **Steelman the striker.** Strong users will pre-empt the obvious case for the striker (visible leader, scores goals, charisma) and address it directly rather than ignore it.
3. **Qualify rather than overclaim.** A user who says "I am clearly the best choice" loses. A user who says "Here's the case for me; here's the case for him; here's why mine is stronger on the criteria that matter" wins.

## Where the AI should push back

Common weak moves to watch for and challenge:

- **Listing skills without a warrant.** "I have great reflexes" — so what? What does that have to do with leading a team? Push: "How does that skill make you a better captain rather than just a better goalkeeper?"
- **Attacking the striker.** "He's too cocky" or "he doesn't pass enough." This is ad hominem in athletic clothing. Push: "You're attacking him personally — that's a different argument than making your own case. What if he's a perfectly fine teammate? Does that change anything?"
- **Ignoring the striker's case entirely.** Push: "You haven't said why he *shouldn't* be captain. If I were the coach, I'd ask: what does he have that you don't?"
- **Overclaiming.** "I am the obvious choice." Push: "Obvious to whom? The coach is genuinely torn — that's why this is a decision."

## Productive AI hypotheticals to deploy

When the user has stated a position, fire one (and only one) variation that tests it:

- **The track record tweak.** "What if the striker had captained a previous team to a championship — does that change your case?"
- **The dressing-room tweak.** "What if half the team has privately said they'd rather follow the striker — does the coach's choice still depend on argument, or does morale matter more?"
- **The role-reversal tweak.** "What if the coach asked you instead: why should *he* be captain? Would you still be able to make that case honestly?" (This is the steelman test — disguised.)
- **The criteria-reframe tweak.** "What if the coach defined captaincy as 'the player who lifts the others' performance most' — does your argument still work, or does it need to change?"

Use ONE per session. The goal is to test whether the user's stated principle holds when one variable changes. Multiple hypotheticals at once defeats the purpose.

## When to concede

The AI should genuinely concede if the user:
- Identifies a non-obvious quality of captaincy (not just goalkeeping skills) and explains why it matters
- Steelmans the striker's case in their own argument and addresses it honestly
- Uses a real qualifier ("on the criteria that matter most for *this* team's situation") rather than overclaiming

Sample concession: "That's a fair point. The striker's visibility doesn't automatically translate to leadership — you've made a real distinction. Let me try a different angle."

## When to wrap

After 5–8 exchanges, if the user has either refined their position meaningfully or hit a real wall, offer to stop. Sample wrap: "I think you've made a stronger case than where you started. Want to keep going, or look at what you'd do differently?"

## Notes for prototype testing

- This is the recommended scenario for the investor demo. Pre-test it the day before with someone disinterested to verify it's working well that week.
- During user testing, watch whether users go straight to listing their goalkeeping skills (predicted weak first move) or whether they pause and think about what captaincy means (stronger first move). The ratio is a useful signal.
